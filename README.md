# Learning Predator–Prey Interactions from Real-World Animal Behavior 🦈🐟

This repository accompanies our paper on learning predator–prey dynamics from synthetic and real-world animal trajectories using **Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL)**.

The framework models predator and prey with separate role-specific policies and discriminators and applies alternating co-training to their conflicting behavioral objectives. Synthetic Couzin-based demonstrations are used for controlled verification before applying the same architecture to trajectories reconstructed from aquarium recordings.

## Abstract

<p align="justify">
Collective motion in swarm systems exhibits complex behavioral patterns emerging from local interactions and external influences. Learning policies that reproduce such dynamics remains challenging, especially in predator–prey settings where heterogeneous roles interact under conflicting objectives. This work investigates whether predator–prey behavior can be learned directly from demonstrations using a role-asymmetric adversarial imitation learning framework. Separate modular policies for predator and prey are trained against role-specific discriminators, while self-supervised transition encoders provide compact latent representations of trajectory dynamics. Policy optimization is performed with OpenAI Evolution Strategies and alternates between the two behavioral roles. The framework is first verified on clean synthetic trajectories generated from a Couzin-based swarm model and is subsequently applied to trajectories reconstructed from real aquarium recordings. The learned policies reproduce basic collective motion and parts of the observed predator–prey interaction structure, while sustained pursuit and coordinated avoidance remain challenging. The results highlight both the potential and the limitations of learning competitive multi-agent behavior directly from biological demonstrations.
</p>

## Further Analysis

In addition to evaluating imitation quality at the trajectory and swarm level, we analyze the learned modular policies to better understand their interaction structure. The pairwise interaction network (PIN) captures the steering response induced by individual neighbors, while the attention network (AN) determines how strongly each neighbor contributes to the aggregated action.

<p align="center">
  <img src="images/projects/maps.png" alt="Pairwise interaction and attention maps" width="600">
</p>

The policy maps provide a local view of the learned interaction rules. Predator responses exhibit directional structure with respect to nearby prey, while the corresponding attention maps indicate which spatial regions contribute most strongly to the final action. These visualizations complement the trajectory-level evaluation by revealing interaction patterns that are not directly visible from global swarm metrics alone.

<p align="center">
  <img src="images/projects/traj.png" alt="Trajectory prediction" width="600">
</p>

Short-horizon trajectory rollouts are used to compare policy-generated motion with expert trajectories from identical initial configurations. Repeated stochastic rollouts visualize the variability of the learned policies and show how heading deviations accumulate over time.

<p align="center">
  <img src="images/projects/network.png" alt="Attention network analysis" width="600">
</p>

The attention network further enables agent-level interpretation of the learned interaction structure. Neighbor-specific attention weights indicate which individuals contribute most strongly to the focal agent's current decision and provide an additional perspective on information flow within the swarm.

## Method Overview

The framework uses the same focal-centered state representation for synthetic and biological trajectories. Each focal–neighbor pair is represented by relative position and relative velocity, while the prey representation additionally contains a binary predator flag.

The main components are:

- separate predator and prey policies based on pairwise interaction and attention networks,
- separate transition encoders for compact latent trajectory representations,
- role-specific Wasserstein discriminators,
- OpenAI-ES for gradient-free policy optimization, and
- alternating optimization of predator and prey policies.

The framework is evaluated progressively on:

1. **Couzin prey-only demonstrations** for single-species verification,
2. **Couzin predator–prey demonstrations** for controlled multi-species verification, and
3. **biological predator–prey trajectories** reconstructed from aquarium recordings.

<br>

## Evaluation

Behavior is evaluated using complementary collective-motion and predator-related metrics:

- polarization,
- angular momentum,
- mean nearest-neighbor distance,
- predator distance to the prey centroid,
- predator distance to the nearest prey, and
- escape alignment.

These metrics are complemented by policy-map analysis and trajectory-level comparisons.
